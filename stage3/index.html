Corrections learned from reward around the frozen four-expert model

The released model is frozen. A small network learns bounded corrections to its commands from the outcome alone, in a physics simulation of clean_plate_with_eraser. On 29 scenes it never trained in, task success rises from 29 % to 63 %.

Learning curve

Every fifth training episode runs without exploration noise and stays out of training, alternately in a training scene and in one of 32 validation scenes set aside from them. The critic's gradient reaches the actor from about 2,500 episodes on. 24,000 episodes take 2.9 hours on 30 CPU processes and one GPU.

The setup

  • Scene. Two hands, a table, a plate and an eraser in MuJoCo. Plate, eraser and table are placed per recording from the stereo cameras and the recorded fingertips. 301 recordings of the task give 240 training scenes, 32 validation scenes and 29 held-out scenes; the held-out scenes are the model's own held-out episodes. Friction, masses and five scene corrections were calibrated on 60 training scenes so that the operator's commands complete the task as often as possible.
  • Frozen model. The released four-expert model reads the recorded observations and re-predicts every 8 frames. Its commands are the reference. It does not see the simulated scene.
  • Corrections. Every 0.2 s a network with two hidden layers of 256 units (the actor) outputs 12 numbers: a position offset of each hand base, within ±2 cm per axis, and three finger synergies per hand, within ±0.75 standard deviations, both rate-limited.
  • What the actor sees. The frozen model's contact and phase read-outs, the plate and the eraser relative to the hands, the simulated fingertip forces, the hand posture, and how far hands and fingers are from their commands. Nothing that identifies the recording.
  • Learning. Facet-0's online adaptation with the robot replaced by the simulator: twin critics, a readiness gate on the critic before any of its gradient reaches the actor, and imitation of the corrections of successful episodes with a weight that falls from 1.0 to 0.3. The critic also sees the reference motion and the simulator's object state; it is not deployed.
  • Reward. Dense progress, at most 1 per episode: plate lifted, eraser lifted, plate held during the wipe, wiped path, plate set down.
  • Perturbation. Every training episode shifts both objects by up to 1 cm, turns the eraser by up to 8° and scales friction and mass by 0.7 to 1.3. The evaluation uses 20 fixed perturbations per scene, the same for every policy.

  • Fewer wipes that are too short, fewer failed pick-ups. The left table sorts every held-out run by how it ended.
  • It is a reaction to the scene, not a fixed adjustment. Replacing the actor with its own mean correction, or with one mean for the wiping phase and one for the rest, recovers a small part of the gain. The actor is +23.6 points [+14.3, +32.2] above the better of the two.
  • The critic's gradient is what makes it work. The matched control keeps the schedule, the exploration and the imitation of successful episodes and removes only the critic's gradient. It ends below the frozen model at 14 %.
  • A second training run confirms the size. Another seed reaches 58 % (+28.6 points [+19.7, +37.2]). It picks the plate up more often and wipes further, as the first run does; it does not drop the plate less often, so that part of the first run's gain is not a stable effect.

What failed first

  • First design: the actor also read the frozen model's pooled token. Success in the training scenes rose from 24 % to 50 %; on the held-out scenes it went from 43 % to 37 %. The token is computed from the recorded cameras, so it identifies the recording, and the actor had learned one set of corrections per training scene.
  • Second design: no token, but the actor still saw the reference motion. Stopped after 6,550 episodes with the training scenes at 45 % and the validation scenes flat at 34 to 38 %. The reference motion identifies the recording as well. In the final design only the critic sees it.
  • First task definition. "Plate back on the table" required a tilt under 45°, which cut through the pose the model plate comes to rest in, and the wipe path counted contact jitter. Both were replaced. The numbers of the first two designs are under the first definition.
  • The held-out scenes were looked at once before the final run, to diagnose the first design. Every later choice was made on the 32 validation scenes, and the final actor is the last checkpoint, not a selected one.

Training curves of stages 1 and 2

The three stages follow Facet-0: supervised training, offline refinement with a critic, online corrections. Stage 3 above was applied directly to the stage-1 model. Stage 2 was run on an earlier flow-matching policy, gave no clear gain, and is not part of the released model.

Limits

  • A rough simulation, not a robot. Simulated fingertip forces peak at about 200 N where the recorded ones peak at about 40 N; the plate is a disc on a stem; the arms are left out. The operator's own commands complete the task in a third of the runs, so most failures of the frozen model are failures of the reconstruction. What the corrections learned is in part how to do the task in this simulation.
  • The frozen model does not close the loop. It reads the recorded observations; only the corrections react to the simulated scene.
  • The actor is given the object poses from the simulator. On a robot they would have to come from the cameras.
  • The critic sees the simulator's state. It is used in training only.
  • One task, two training runs.
